*You've been prescribed an antidepressant. Your therapist thinks it's burnout. Your doctor says your labs are "normal." But something still feels deeply off — and you can't shake the feeling that everyone is missing something.*

You're not imagining it. And you might be right.

If you're experiencing a constellation of symptoms — anxiety that came out of nowhere, mood swings you don't recognize, sleep that's falling apart, brain fog, heart palpitations, unexplained weight gain, irregular periods — there's a very real possibility that what you're experiencing is **perimenopause.**

## How often is perimenopause misdiagnosed?

In a [2025 national survey](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20251113649089/en/Nearly-40-of-Women-Say-They-were-Misdiagnosed-During-Perimenopause-National-Survey-Reveals) of more than 1,000 U.S. women aged 30 to 60, commissioned by the hormone therapy company Biote, **nearly 40% said they felt misdiagnosed** when they sought care for perimenopause symptoms. Among women who had been prescribed medication for a condition like depression, a similar share felt they had not been appropriately diagnosed.

That survey measures women's own experiences rather than a confirmed clinical misdiagnosis rate. But it matches what I hear from patients every week: many women going through one of the most significant physiological transitions of their lives are treated for stress or mood disorders while the hormonal piece goes unexamined.

## Why is perimenopause so often mistaken for anxiety or depression?

Perimenopause is missed because it starts earlier than most people expect, its mood symptoms overlap almost completely with anxiety and depression, and standard lab work doesn't include hormones. On top of that, two decades of caution about hormone therapy left many clinicians less comfortable evaluating it.

**Perimenopause starts earlier than most people expect.** The popular understanding is that menopause happens "around 50." What's less widely known is that **perimenopause — the transitional phase that precedes it — can begin as early as the late 30s and typically starts in the early to mid-40s.** It can last anywhere from 4 to 10 years. Many women experiencing perimenopausal symptoms are in their early 40s, an age when most clinicians aren't thinking hormones first.

**The symptoms mimic anxiety and depression closely.** Declining and fluctuating estrogen affects serotonin, dopamine, and GABA — the same neurotransmitters targeted by antidepressants and anti-anxiety medications. If you're only looking at mood symptoms and not at the bigger picture, they can be very hard to tell apart.

**Standard lab work often comes back "normal."** A standard TSH (thyroid) test might be normal. A basic metabolic panel might be normal. A CBC might be normal. If no one is looking at hormones — and interpreting them in the context of your cycle and symptoms — the hormonal picture simply doesn't show up. Women are then told everything looks fine, when the most relevant data was never collected.

**The medical system has historically undertreated women's hormonal health.** Following the misinterpretation of the Women's Health Initiative study in 2002, hormone-related conversations were effectively suppressed in primary care for two decades. Many clinicians trained in that era simply aren't comfortable evaluating perimenopausal hormonal status. The FDA's removal of the black box warning from HRT in late 2025 is helping to change the culture, but clinical training takes time to catch up.

**Women are conditioned to normalize their symptoms.** How many women have been told that fatigue, mood changes, and sleep disruption are "just part of being a busy woman"? Many delay seeking care — and when they do, they're already primed to accept "stress" as an explanation.

## What are the symptoms of perimenopause?

Perimenopause rarely shows up as just one thing. It's a cluster of symptoms across several body systems — menstrual, mood, sleep, cognitive and physical — that each have a plausible alternative explanation on their own. Together, in a woman in her late 30s to 50s, they tell a different story.

- **Menstrual changes:** cycles that get shorter (from 28 days to 24 or 25), then longer, then irregular; heavier or lighter bleeding; skipped periods. These reflect fluctuating estrogen and progesterone as ovarian reserve declines.
- **Mood changes:** anxiety that feels different from anything you've had before — more physical, more sudden, sometimes with heart palpitations; irritability out of proportion to circumstances; a flat, low mood that doesn't lift the way it used to.
- **Sleep disruption:** trouble falling asleep, waking at 2 or 3 AM, waking unrefreshed, and night sweats that may be mild enough you don't recognize them as hot flashes. Poor sleep alone worsens mood, memory and metabolism.
- **Brain fog and memory:** thinking "through mud," word-finding trouble, difficulty concentrating, short-term memory lapses — tied to estrogen's role in the brain's memory centers.
- **Physical symptoms:** joint pain, new or worsening headaches and migraines, palpitations, vaginal dryness, bladder urgency, skin changes and hair thinning.
- **Weight changes:** new abdominal fat and a metabolism that no longer responds the way it used to, driven by estrogen's role in fat distribution and insulin sensitivity.

Not every woman experiences every symptom. But the pattern across these categories should prompt a hormone conversation — not just an antidepressant prescription.

## How can you tell if it's perimenopause, anxiety, depression, or your thyroid?

The biggest clues are your age, whether your periods are changing, and whether symptoms follow your cycle. These conditions can also overlap — you can have perimenopause and depression at the same time — so the goal is a complete evaluation, not choosing one label.

## Which tests should you ask for if you suspect perimenopause?

There's no single test that confirms perimenopause, because hormone levels fluctuate so much that one blood draw can look normal. Testing is still useful: it adds context to your symptoms and rules out other causes, especially thyroid disease.

**Track your symptoms against your cycle first.** If your anxiety, mood swings, or sleep problems follow a pattern — worse in the week before your period, or at specific points in your cycle — that pattern is itself useful diagnostic information. An app like Clue or a simple paper calendar works.

**Then ask about these markers** — ideally with a provider who knows how to interpret them in the context of your cycle phase:

- Estradiol
- FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone)
- Progesterone (around day 21 of the cycle if you're still having periods)
- Total and free testosterone
- DHEA-S
- Cortisol
- A full thyroid panel: TSH, free T3, free T4 and TPO antibodies

**Don't skip the thyroid.** Hypothyroidism mimics perimenopause almost symptom for symptom: fatigue, weight gain, mood changes, brain fog, hair thinning, feeling cold. Hashimoto's thyroiditis affects women far more often than men and is frequently underdiagnosed. A full thyroid panel — not just TSH — is worth doing before or alongside a hormone evaluation.

## What should you do if you think it's perimenopause?

Write down your symptoms, get your hormones and thyroid checked, and find a provider who takes the possibility seriously. If you're already on an antidepressant, keep taking it and bring this up with your prescriber — don't stop it on your own.

1. **Document your symptoms.** Write down what you're experiencing, when it started, how it relates to your cycle, and what you've already tried. This history is gold in a clinical conversation.
2. **Get your hormones tested.** Don't wait for someone to offer it. Ask explicitly, or start with an at-home [hormone testing kit](https://www.wellpharmapharmacy.com/hormone-kits) to get baseline data before your appointment.
3. **Find a provider who takes it seriously.** If your doctor attributes everything to stress without looking at your hormonal status, it's reasonable to seek a second opinion — from a menopause specialist, an integrative medicine provider, or a pharmacist trained in women's hormone health.
4. **Don't stop current medications on your own.** Antidepressants and anti-anxiety medications can still be the right treatment, and stopping suddenly can cause withdrawal and relapse. Any change should be made with your prescriber.
5. **Know that treatment options exist.** If perimenopause is driving your symptoms, options include hormone replacement therapy (with updated safety guidance since the FDA's 2025 label change), targeted supplements, lifestyle changes, and for some women, compounded bioidentical hormones tailored to their hormone profile.
6. **Don't accept "your labs are normal" as a final answer.** If your labs didn't include hormones, they can't tell you whether hormones are the problem.

## You Know Your Body

Women are extraordinarily good at knowing when something is wrong with their bodies. The instinct that says "this anxiety is different," or "I've never felt this way before" — that instinct is usually worth listening to.

Perimenopause isn't a weakness, and it isn't "just stress." It's a profound biological transition that deserves to be recognized, evaluated properly, and treated with the same seriousness as any other significant physiological event.
